| TEACHER OVERVIEW — L5

LESSON 5  —  Detail Questions — Locating Explicit Information
P1+P9: Question → Keywords → Locate → Read → Answer → Where? → Which words prove it?

Objective
By the end of this lesson the student finds explicit answers in the text and provides the exact sentence as evidence.
Key insight
"A detail question has one correct answer and it is in the text — word for word. Students who paraphrase too much, or add their own knowledge, always lose marks. The answer is there. Find it. Write it."
Timing plan

Common mistakes to watch for
· Student writes their own opinion instead of text words — "Only from the text" must be a mantra
· Student gives a long answer when the question says "ONE answer" — teach them to count
· Student answers Q4 with too many words and breaks the grammar of the starter sentence
· Student cannot find the evidence — remind: "Underline the proof sentence first, then write" |

| THE HOMEWORK QUESTION
I     Most students receive homework every day. Some experts argue that homework is essential for learning, while others believe it causes more harm than good. The debate has become more serious in recent years, as studies show that students today spend almost twice as much time on homework as students did 20 years ago. However, research suggests that more homework does not always mean better results.
II     Dr. Chloe Martin, a professor of education at Lyon University in France, studied 5,000 secondary school students over a period of three years. She found that students who spent more than two hours a night on homework were no more successful in exams than those who spent only 45 minutes. "The key is not the amount of homework, but the quality," says Dr. Martin.
III     Not everyone agrees with Dr. Martin's findings. Professor Alex Nkosi of the University of Cape Town argues that homework builds important habits, such as planning and self-discipline. However, both researchers agree on one point: homework should never take more than 90 minutes per evening for secondary school students. |

| THE SLEEP DEBATE
Scientists have been debating the question of how much sleep teenagers need for many years. A new study by Dr. Fatima Hasan at Cairo University examined 3,000 high school students over four years. She found that students who slept for nine hours performed significantly better in exams than those who slept for six hours or less. However, not all experts agree. Professor Yuki Tanaka of Tokyo University argues that the quality of sleep is more important than the quantity. "A teenager who sleeps for seven hours without interruption benefits more than one who sleeps for ten hours but wakes frequently," he says. |
