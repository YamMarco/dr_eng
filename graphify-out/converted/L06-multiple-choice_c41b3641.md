| TEACHER OVERVIEW — L6

LESSON 6  —  Multiple Choice — Elimination Strategy
Read all options before the text · Cross out wrong answers · Evidence for your choice

Objective
By the end of this lesson the student eliminates clearly wrong multiple choice options systematically and finds text evidence before confirming their answer.
Key insight
"Most students read option i) and if it looks right, they choose it and move on. The best students read ALL FOUR options, cross out the ones they can prove wrong, and only then look for evidence for the remaining one. This prevents choosing a distractor."
Timing plan

Common mistakes to watch for
· Student chooses the first option that mentions a word from the question — classic distractor trap
· Student does not check all four options before deciding
· Student picks an option that has true information but answers the WRONG question
· Student writes their elimination work in the margin — good habit, reinforce it |

| THE HOMEWORK QUESTION
I     Most students receive homework every day. Some experts argue that homework is essential for learning, while others believe it causes more harm than good. The debate has become more serious in recent years, as studies show that students today spend almost twice as much time on homework as students did 20 years ago. However, research suggests that more homework does not always mean better results.
II     Dr. Chloe Martin, a professor of education at Lyon University in France, studied 5,000 secondary school students over a period of three years. She found that students who spent more than two hours a night on homework were no more successful in exams than those who spent only 45 minutes. "The key is not the amount of homework, but the quality," says Dr. Martin.
III     Not everyone agrees with Dr. Martin's findings. Professor Alex Nkosi of the University of Cape Town argues that homework builds important habits, such as planning and self-discipline. However, both researchers agree on one point: homework should never take more than 90 minutes per evening for secondary school students. |
| --- |
| TEACHER — I DO
I DO — teacher models elimination on Q1:
Q1: "What do we learn from paragraph II about Dr. Martin's study?"
STEP 1: Read all options. Write ✓ maybe / ✗ probably wrong next to each.
i) "Why she chose Lyon University" — probably wrong. The paragraph does not discuss her reasons.
ii) "How many students she studied" — possible. Numbers in questions → P2.
iii) "What she argues about all schools" — check paragraph II. Not mentioned there.
iv) "What she discovered about time spent on homework" — possible. Her finding is there.
STEP 2: Cross out i) and iii). STEP 3: ii) vs iv). Go to paragraph II. P3: Dr. Martin → her paragraph.
STEP 4: "students who spent more than two hours... were no more successful than those who spent only 45 minutes." → iv). |

| THE SCREEN TIME DEBATE
Parents and teachers have been worried about screen time for many years. Most experts agree that too much screen time can harm children's sleep and social skills. However, a new study from Stanford University suggests that screen time is not always harmful. Dr. Emily Park, who led the study, examined 4,500 children aged between 8 and 12. She found that children who spent up to one hour a day on creative screen activities — such as drawing or coding programmes — showed better problem-solving skills than those who spent no time on screens. |

Evidence: "creative screen activities — such as drawing or coding programmes." |
